How do I consider my research? and what is Efficient AI?

Yes, my research focuses on intelligence, and my official title is Assistant Professor.

However, when it comes to my self-identification, what we refer to in most cases is one’s occupation.

What is an occupation? An occupation is the work people engage in to make a living.

For me, working at Westlake University carries a far more profound meaning: it is my vocation. It is the very way I exist as a human being.

At the fundamental level, even my professional work is part of all my conscious activities, guided and framed by my world outlook, outlook on life, and values. Long before I took this position, my thinking as an individual was shaped and inspired by Enlightenment thinkers (Jean-Jacques Rousseau), German classical philosophy (Immanuel Kant), contemporary art (Marcel Duchamp), and Karl Marx.

Existing as a means is only one part of my being. I hope this part will take up less and less space in my life, until ultimately, I exist purely as an end in myself.

None of the above has a direct link to AI or my specific research direction (though they are intrinsically connected at the fundamental level). Yet none of us exists as a collection of isolated attributes; we exist as an integrated whole. These ideas and influences profoundly shape how I view current technologies, including AI, how I work at Westlake University, how I lead my research group, and what kind of papers I choose to publish, among many other things. For this reason, beyond my core research work, I have maintained a long-term focus on philosophy and sociology. While these are labeled as academic disciplines that seem to stand parallel to computer science and AI — one falling into the humanities and social sciences, the other into science and engineering — I lean toward the view held by Auguste Comte and many philosophers: these disciplines do not stand on an equal footing in parallel, but rather exist in a hierarchical, inclusive relationship. I study them not out of curiosity / hobby, but because they serve a critical purpose: they help me understand the fundamental nature of the world and of myself. Hierarchically and ontologically, this pursuit sits at a higher level than my AI research. The former is about ends and existence, while the latter is about means and livelihood.

My own work accounts for only a small fraction of the broader field of AI (we all must maintain focused given our limited time and energy). However, at the level of thinking and inquiry, my attention extends far beyond the scope covered in my published papers. Broadly speaking, I focus on two core areas: first, how AI can serve as a better information processing tool (where "better" is defined as stronger performance at a lower cost, grounded in instrumental rationality); and second, AI ethics (grounded in value rationality).

To elaborate on how AI can be a better information processing tool: AI as it exists today is built on computer systems. What it essentially does is deploy models onto hardware, and the work we do acts as a "bridge" between the two. This bridge is structured in layers: the algorithm layer, the system layer, and the hardware layer. Each layer comes with different potential gains and risks. During my PhD, my work focused primarily on the algorithm layer; now, my research spans both the algorithm and system layers.

We place no restrictions on the upper-layer models and tasks we work on; we pursue cutting-edge topics as they emerge. This is why you will see that our work has covered a wide range of tasks, including classification, style transfer, super-resolution, text-to-image generation, large video models, robot manipulation, and single-exposure imaging, among others. Fundamentally, this is because the processing tool for all these tasks is the neural network model. Our core research objects are inherently models and hardware, and which specific task the model is applied to is ultimately of secondary relevance to our work.
