Research Statement

Huan Wang
Westlake University, Hangzhou, China

1. Brief Introduction

Yes, I am doing "AI research", as a Tenure-Track Assistant Professor at Westlake University, Hangzhou, China. My group, ENCODE Lab, focuses on Efficient AI, an area trying to build efficient and reliable AI systems that are scalable and self-improving.

2. What is Efficient AI?

Efficient AI is more about computing systems, at the implementation level of AI. If we look at the past history of AI (roughly starting from Turing's great paper in 1950), we might be bezzled by the fact that AI is so interdisplenary topic that has been stuided so broadly by many diverse groups. They try to answer what is human-like intelligence. These questions I call "the AI modeling problem". Just, as is well known, computers based on transistors are well developed. With sufficient data, a data-driven paradigm now is demonating. Efficient AI, the way I see it, is treated in the context of computing systems, namely, the implementation level instead of the modeling level. A fact to see this is that: many researchers in efficient AI may approach the problem by optimizing the memory access, where they do not even need a trained model.

Efficient AI is like a "bridge"; tradeoff is everywhere. What we are doing is deploy AI models (deep neural networks) on hardware, and the work we do acts as a "bridge" between the two. Efficient AI is bascially about getting the model training and deployment, and inference more efficient. We are more focused on model deployment and inference (since we do not have much Comput resource for training). Efficiency is never a free lunch. So we need to make tradeoffs between different aspects: time, space (memory), energy, money, accuracy, etc. A good man in effcient AI is those who are familiar with the model (such as deep network architecture), the algorithm (how model is used), and the underlying hardware (such as GPU/CPU/NPU/FPGA/ASIC, etc.). namey, a full-stack guy.

Computing systems are hierarchical: the algorithm level, the model level, the system level, and the hardware level. We can get efficiency gains from each level - this is why you may see researchers from different backgrounds all claim they are doing efficient AI. But different levels come with different potential gains and worloads - developping a fast AI inference algorithm (such as INT4 quantization) is easy (in 1 month), but the gain is also relatviely limited (e.g., 2-3x speedup). During my Ph.D. period, I focused primarily on the algorithm level (I wrote Python, rarely touching C/C++/CUDA); now, my research spans all the spectrum of the computing systems (Python/C/C++/CUDA/Triton/ASM/etc. are all our choices, as is necessary).

Efficient AI is less about the task. AI tasks are unifying in the past several years (thanks to transformers and some general representation learning paradigms). Efficient AI is therefore less about specific tasks. We are dealing with deep models, which are typically transformers. And the data now is all token flow. As a result, we place no restrictions on the specific tasks we work on; we pursue new topics as they emerge (in Chinese: "追热点"). This is why you may see that my works have covered a wide range of tasks, including image classification, style transfer, super-resolution, text-to-image generation, large video models, robot manipulation, and SCI (snapshot compressed imaging), among others.

3. The Problem Should be Considered as One - in a Technical Meaning

If we ask "What is the next big problem in AI now?", it is no surprise to hear many would say: "Obviously, how to achieve AGI or Strong AI is the next big problem in AI". The success of LLMs are so astonishing that many people are convinced that AGI or Strong AI is just around the corner, for the first time in history. The path leading us here is said in many different ways, but the core is the same: "Scaling Law" - scaling the model and feed it with more data, supported with more GPUs. Finger crossed: When the model/data/GPUs are enough, intelligence/magic will "emerge" on its own. This path is called the empirical path, data fitting, trial-and-error, etc.

I also believe the empirical path - i.e., fitting the data can lead to intelligence, but the point is: to which level? The empirical path can lead us to a certain distance, while I do not believe this trial-and-error approach can lead us for that far. An example to illustrate this point: Before Claude Shannon formalizes the mathamatical definition of "information", radio, wireless communication already existed for quite a while, just in the dark. Without information theory, we probably can still listen to music via radio at present; yet Turbo code and LDPC code will very unlikely appear, and we cannot have the Internet as we know it today. Big data will also very unlikely be possible, let alone the current AI boom. I deem this the power of a good theory, something arising from the empirical data but beyond - "Nothing is more practical than a good theory (in the long run)", if we still remember it.

Speaking of a good theory, if we look at the AI history (starting from Turing's great paper in 1950), we can see many great minds from a vaerity of fields have been trying to answer the AI modeling problem: Philosophy, Psychology, Neuroscience, Cognitive Science, in addition to the computer science and engineering. Now, it seems the room is crowed with people of only a few kinds. Too boring - the computer guys are the dominant force in the AI field; among them, LLM guys are the dominant (Yann LeCun even argued: Young Researchers should avoid LLMs). No wonder, some said "LLM sucks the oxgen out of the room".

No need to think it so hard, we will see that AI problem is clearly not just about computers and engineering. Opinions from all disciplines should be considered (in a formal/mathematical manner, not citing their sayings like quoting a maxim of Aristotle) when developing technical methods or systems.

Hereby, I believe that the problem should be considered as one.

4. The Problem Should be Considered as One - in a Broader Meaning

Being a researcher is an occupation, a means of livelihood. It is also a vocation, a way of “Dasein”, which should be examined.

On many occasions, we are accustomed to defining ourselves by a school, a department, a narrow field. I deem this as a necessity brought by industrial division of labor, not the way things ought to be, especially when what we study is AI.

AI is mostly treated in the domain of science and technology, while from the very beginning, its context and meaning are much richer. No discipline in the past has been like this: The creature is to be like the creator. Even if we refrain from a religious perspective such as "we are doing something like playing God", nor can we deny that AI research is probably the most important research of all time, if we take it really seriously.

This means we should not, and cannot, consider it a problem that is purely technical; nor deprive others (including those who do not even code) of the interest in the problem. At least, many AI researchers are electrified by the idea of a super intelligent system (AGI/Strong AI) that aims to replace common humans laboring in various fields, as many pieces of evidence have projected. But have we ever asked if they want to be replaced? Or, more broadly, why the technology has to keep going? Where will we end up?

Now, let’s just look at the builders of AI. As human beings, AI researchers must also answer questions like the meaning of life, and the deeper significance of doing AI. We can explain the technical motivation of a method, but we must also explain the motivation of the people who build this technology — the “hidden motivation”, I would call it. From my even limited experience, I have found that this hidden motivation determines the external outcomes, and also shapes the technical motivation itself.

If we consider the profound importance of the AI problem and the fact that the main producers of current AI papers are actually a very young group (Ph.D./M.S. or even undergrad/high school students), then in reality we are entrusting a critical issue that concerns the fate of humanity to a group of young people. I am not saying they lack capability; many are talented as we have seen; rather, it seems they have been shouldering a burden far beyond their years - this is the price. Anxiety is pervasive — something I can clearly sense within my own group — and many are burning out. This state itself is non-technical. It hides behind the papers and influences the technical outcomes we see, yet it is not fully discussed.

In my group, I try to help my students to keep up with the pace and meanwhile seeking ways to "jump out", with a kind of the "old-fashioned motivation. Two examples of this "old-fashioned motivation: 1) the address by Albert Einstein in 1918: Principles of Research, and also the story how Records of the Grand Historian () was written by a father and son: Sima Tan and Sima Qian, two grand historians in 100 BC.

Here we say, motivation matters; perspective matters.

Some may argue these problems belong to philosophy and sociology, psychologists, etc., while we are computer scientists or engineers. They are not our areas. I think this is a valid argument, yet merely in a narrow sense. In a broader sense, we have no other choice but to accept the challenge, once it is realized that we cannot really outsource our own personal questions — the meaning of life, the purpose of existence, what kind of work, research, family, friendship, and relationships we want — to people called “scientists”, “philosophers”, “sociologists”, or “psychologists”, simply because they are deemed more “expert” in these areas than we are.

Hereby, I believe that the problem should be considered as one.
